Your deal stages should describe how a buyer actually moves from first conversation to signed deal. This is my opinion, not a HubSpot rule. And to be clear up front: HubSpot's default stage names are a perfectly good starting point. I don't mark anyone down for using them.

## Why it matters

Stages are where your forecast comes from. Each stage has a probability, and HubSpot uses it to weight the pipeline. Stage history is also how you see where deals stall and how long each step takes.

If a stage doesn't match a real step in the process, all of that gets fuzzy. Reps put deals wherever feels close. Two reps use the same stage to mean different things. The forecast looks precise but isn't.

## How to check it

1. Go to **Settings > Objects > Deals > Pipelines** and open each deal pipeline.
2. For each stage, ask: what has the **buyer** done or agreed to by the time a deal gets here?
3. Then ask a rep to walk you through their last few won deals. Do the stages line up with what actually happened?

A clear test: if two people on your team would put the same deal in different stages, the stage needs a sharper definition.

## What good looks like

- **Each stage is a milestone, not an activity.** "Decision maker agreed to a proposal review" is a milestone. "Sent follow-up email" is an activity, and activities belong in tasks and the timeline.
- **Each stage has a short entry rule** that everyone on the team would agree with.
- **Default or template names are fine** if they fit. "Appointment scheduled" or "Contract sent" work for lots of teams. Clones of one pipeline that share the same names are fine too.

## How to fix it

1. Write down the real steps your buyers take, from first meeting to signature. Usually five to nine is plenty.
2. Map your current stages to those steps. Rename the ones that are close. Remove the ones that are really activities.
3. Write a one-line entry rule for each stage and put it somewhere reps will see it, like your sales playbook.
4. Prefer renaming a stage over deleting it. Before deleting one, move its deals to the right stage and check the workflows and reports that use it.
5. Revisit probabilities once the stages mean something specific.

Don't rename stages just to make them sound better. If the defaults describe your process, keep them and spend the time on entry rules instead.
